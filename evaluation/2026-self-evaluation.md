# 2026 Annual Self Evaluation

**David Molik**

Associate Professor and Department Head, Open Publishing Exchange

Kansas State University Libraries

**Working draft for review — revised October 9, 2026**

This self-evaluation follows the expectations and weights in the September 29, 2026 signed UPP sent to HR. It draws primarily on work through September 23 and remains open for later-year updates and supervisor review.

## Overview

My goal as Head of OPE is to build scholarly communication services the Libraries can sustain. I understand sustainability as financial support for our commitments, operational practices that share responsibility, maintainable technical infrastructure, and research that helps us assess and improve our work. These concerns connect publishing, repositories, open education, and research-data services.

Network building supports this work across the department, campus, and professional communities. My focus is on turning relationships and ideas into shared practices that help researchers, students, and colleagues. The sections below describe that work against each UPP expectation and reflect on what I am learning from it.

## Professional Conduct

### Interact cooperatively with colleagues, show civility and respect to others with whom you work and interact, show respect for the opinions of others in the exchange of ideas, and demonstrate a willingness to follow appropriate directives from supervisors (C46.1 UH)

**Weight: High.**

I approach leadership as collaborative work in which colleagues' expertise, judgment, and ownership are visible. In OPE, this means seeking agreement about responsibilities, giving substantive feedback, and coordinating decisions with colleagues and supervisors. My proposal mentoring, student-role planning, and delegation of departmental representation reflect this approach. I want colleagues to have both the support and the authority needed to carry their work forward.

**Lessons learned:** Cooperative leadership requires clear expectations as well as openness to other views. I am learning to make the transition from discussion to responsibility more explicit: who will decide, who will carry the work forward, and when we will revisit it. Timely consultation and direct communication help turn collegial relationships into dependable working arrangements.

**Supporting examples:** [Mentoring and staffing examples][mentoring]; [website responsibilities][website-plan].

## Directed Service

### Core responsibilities

**Weight: High.**

My central work as Head of OPE is to bring publishing, repositories, open education, and research-data services into a coherent departmental program. The June OPE Landscape develops the direction established in the 2025 departmental plan and connects service priorities with staffing, funding, technology, and partnerships. Explaining that direction through departmental presentations and the website planning process is part of helping colleagues and campus partners understand what OPE offers and how its services fit together.

Across these areas, I work on the conditions that allow services to continue: publishing agreements and author guidance, open-textbook support, repository policy and development, and platform and preservation choices. Examples include Textbook 2.0 fundraising, the Fungal Genetics Reports platform transition, Pressbooks analysis, and DataCite and CLOCKSS coordination. My publication-reporting work with OVPR and Libraries colleagues also helps develop a clearer picture of campus research activity. These efforts connect practical service needs with the Libraries' longer-term responsibilities for access and stewardship.

**Lessons learned:** Sustainability depends on financial, operational, technical, and intellectual support working together. A promising platform or service idea needs a manageable scope, assigned responsibility, and people prepared to maintain it. The website planning work has reinforced the value of giving colleagues ownership of their service content while coordinating shared departmental decisions. My priority is to carry that clarity into everyday service workflows.

**Supporting examples:** [OPE Landscape][landscape]; [publishing and infrastructure activities][register]; [website plan][website-plan]; [identifier, preservation, and reporting work][deep-review]; [publication-data refresh][completed-refresh].

### Lead and coordinate Libraries research initiatives, support grant development, and foster research collaborations as Library Lead Research Officer.

**Weight: High.**

As Library Lead Research Officer, I help connect the Libraries with research initiatives while there is still room to shape data stewardship, publication, staffing, and costs. This work involves developing relationships, identifying opportunities, bringing collaborators together, and helping turn an initial idea into a project that partners can evaluate. The formal agreement for this role provides a basis for proposal development, scoping, writing, and budgeting.

My work has included defining an OPE data-support component with Cassandra Olds, developing Sunderland operating models and partnerships, and convening colleagues around an IUSE educational-research concept. Taking responsibility for the Grant Support for Library Staff guide also places a continuing research-support resource within this role. Together, these activities make library expertise available earlier in research planning and help colleagues develop their own research leadership.

**Lessons learned:** Research development is most useful when collaborators share an understanding of the question, responsibilities, and resources before committing to delivery. My disciplinary experience helps me recognize the discovery, metadata, reproducibility, and reuse problems researchers face; the library contribution becomes clearer when I explain how a service addresses those problems. I want to deepen that connection through research profiles and Wildcat Scholar, building on earlier committee service and identifying specific contributions with campus partners.

**Supporting examples:** [Role and partnership records][status-review]; [proposal scoping][attachments]; [research consultations][mentoring]; [reappointment recommendations][reappointment].

### Supervise and mentor OPE faculty and staff; support professional development (e.g., grant leadership, national committee participation).

**Weight: Medium.**

I supervise and mentor OPE colleagues by helping clarify responsibilities, providing substantive feedback, and supporting opportunities to develop expertise and leadership. My work with Gwen Sibley's William T. Grant proposal illustrates this approach: I contributed advice on the research question, methods, evidence, and sponsor fit while supporting her ownership of the project. Student-role planning similarly brings meaningful work together with a primary librarian mentor and increasing responsibility.

**Lessons learned:** Supporting colleagues also means being clear and direct about expectations, concerns, and follow-up. The reappointment feedback has prompted me to reflect on how a caring supervisory approach can become too conciliatory. I want to retain that care while addressing challenges earlier and drawing on confidential advice from Tara and trusted colleagues. Clear ownership and agreed follow-up provide a practical way to strengthen both support and accountability.

### Build models for a research center concept integrating compliance, publishing, data, and AI infrastructure.

**Weight: Medium.**

I am developing models for research support that connect compliance, publishing, data management, and AI infrastructure. The OPE Landscape, Sunderland operating-model work, proposal scoping, and regional AI hub discussions provide different settings for considering how these responsibilities could fit together. The purpose is to give partners a realistic account of the services, people, and resources a research center would require.

The OAK-AI Hub support-cost analysis brings research-data management, reporting, and evaluation assumptions into a shared planning model. The Olds proposal and Sunderland work similarly help distinguish routine support from advanced technical and research responsibilities. These models are still developing; they provide a basis for partnership and funding decisions.

**Lessons learned:** Integrated services require explicit handoffs and governance. Bringing functions together in a proposal does not settle who maintains them or how continuing work is supported. I am learning to make service scope, costs, staffing, and responsibility visible early so partners can decide what they can sustain.

**Supporting examples:** [Landscape][landscape]; [proposal models][attachments]; [Sunderland planning][deep-review].

## Non-Directed Service

### Represent Libraries in national research organizations

**Weight: High.**

My participation in national research organizations connects the Libraries with communities developing the standards, infrastructure, and training that researchers use. Through i5k executive and training leadership, AgBioData steering and sustainability work, and my Bioeconomy Information Sharing and Analysis Center advisory relationship, I contribute a perspective that spans disciplinary research and scholarly communication.

This service includes convening colleagues, developing educational programs, and working on the sustainability of shared resources. The i5k education discussions and exploration of a virtual institute, along with AgBioData sustainability leadership and future workshop planning, extend relationships into shared work. AgBioData's Mission and Leadership Award recognized my contribution to that community. Institutional governance work, including LFPS participation, complements these external roles.

**Lessons learned:** Representation is most valuable when relationships lead to responsibilities and exchange of expertise. These communities help me understand needs that OPE will also encounter, while library experience contributes to their discussions of access, stewardship, and continuity. I am learning to focus my participation where that exchange can support lasting work.

**Supporting examples:** [Professional leadership and recognition][service-review]; [governance contributions][deep-review].

### Contribute to national conversations on scholarly communication infrastructure.

**Weight: Medium.**

I contribute to conversations about how scholarly work is reviewed, published, and made accessible through participation in Genome Notes discussions, Peer Community in Genomics, and cyberbiosecurity peer-review activities. These settings let me examine publishing practices from the perspectives of researchers, reviewers, and library publishers.

This engagement helps inform OPE's thinking about publishing options and the infrastructure needed to support them. Community review models are particularly relevant to questions about access, editorial responsibility, and the costs of scholarly communication.

**Lessons learned:** Publishing practices depend on communities as much as platforms. Reviewing and participating in these discussions helps me see how governance, incentives, and expectations affect whether a model works for its users. I bring those questions back to library publishing and author-support work.

**Supporting examples:** [Publishing discussions and review activities][register]; [professional and editorial context][service-review].

### Contribute expertise to community-led efforts advancing open research data, metadata standards, and interoperable scholarly infrastructure.

**Weight: Medium.**

I contribute expertise to community-led work on open research data, metadata standards, and interoperable scholarly infrastructure through i5k, AgBioData, and the Research Data Alliance. Scientific-literature and genome-nomenclature work connects my disciplinary experience with broader questions about how information is described, linked, and reused. Presenting the AgBioData research coordination network to the Research Data Alliance is one way I help connect these communities.

These activities inform OPE's work on identifiers, repositories, preservation, and research-data services. They also provide a way to contribute library concerns about access and stewardship to standards discussions grounded in researchers' everyday practices.

**Lessons learned:** A standard becomes useful through shared understanding and adoption. Technical consistency matters, but so do the explanations, working relationships, and maintenance arrangements that let communities use it. This has reinforced my attention to documentation and interoperability in local service development.

**Supporting examples:** [Standards and working-group activities][register]; [community service][service-review]; [infrastructure development][software-review].

## Research and Creative Activity

### Provide editorial leadership for scholarly publications.

**Weight: High.**

My editorial work supports venues and practices through which scholarly communities share and assess research. I continue editorial leadership in Cyberbiosecurity Quarterly and serve as a guest editor for Cyberinfrastructure and Data Management in Ecology and Evolution. My Peer Community in Genomics work, including the ViroSeek recommendation, adds experience with a community-based review and recommendation model.

These roles connect scholarly judgment with the practical work of library publishing. They help me understand what authors, reviewers, and editors need from publishing services and how editorial coordination supports a credible, accessible publication process.

**Lessons learned:** Sustaining a scholarly venue requires continuing attention to its community and workflow. Editorial responsibility includes helping a process move forward while maintaining standards and clear communication. This experience informs how I think about OPE's relationships with journals and the support needed behind a publication.

**Supporting examples:** [Editorial foundation][evidence-index]; [collection launch][deep-review]; [ViroSeek editorial record][editorial-record].

### Establish a data-driven scholarly communication research lab to study the production, dissemination, and impact of research through large-scale analysis of scholarly data.

**Weight: Medium.**

I am building a research program around how scholarship is produced, discovered, disseminated, and reused. Publication reporting provides an immediate library setting for this work: HAIL, HAIL-local, and Prairie Observatory support collecting, organizing, and examining scholarly records. Developing these methods raises research questions about source coverage, affiliation, provenance, and how measures can inform library and campus decisions. The lab remains in development.

My disciplinary scholarship and research software contribute methods and experience to this agenda. Arthropod and biodiversity genome standards work, FAIR-data scholarship, AgBioData coordination, Barcode Simulator, and PathwayByWire involve related questions about reproducibility, description, and reuse. Seed support and proposals such as Genome Notes, Ag-DEx, FFAR, and K-FORGE have provided opportunities to develop research questions and partnerships around data and publishing infrastructure, with different funding outcomes.

**Lessons learned:** Building tools is one part of building a research program. The more important task is to define questions that connect the tools, retain reproducible methods, and explain what the findings can tell us. Publication reporting has particularly reinforced the importance of understanding missing data and source limitations before interpreting a measure. I want to use that experience to develop studies that connect scholarly-data methods with library choices and researcher needs.

**Supporting examples:** [Scholarship, grants, and proposals][attachments]; [software contributions][software-review]; [completed data refresh][completed-refresh]; [institution profiles][profiles]; [reporting maintenance][maintenance]; [affiliation methods][affiliation]; [implementation planning][index-plan]; [shared architecture][architecture].

### Develop research agenda connecting undergraduate publishing participation with student outcomes (multi-institutional framework).

**Weight: Low.**

I am developing a research agenda on what students gain from participating in scholarly communication and how library publishing can support that learning. The OPE student pathway provides a practical setting for thinking about meaningful work, mentoring, reflection, and increasing responsibility. Course concepts in scholarly-communication research methods and knowledge graphs provide related curriculum ideas.

My IUSE work on maker-based learning has also given me experience in framing an educational-research question and developing a design with collaborators. The specific multi-institutional publishing-and-student-outcomes agenda is at an early stage; its next steps are to define the publishing experience, research questions, and potential partners.

**Lessons learned:** A worthwhile student experience and a study of that experience require different kinds of planning. To understand outcomes, I need to identify what students might learn and how to examine it from the beginning. This is shaping how I connect program design with research and how I approach conversations with potential institutional partners.

**Supporting examples:** [Course concepts][register]; [student pathway and educational-research planning][mentoring].

## Reflection and priorities

The strongest lesson across this year's work is that sustainable services depend on shared responsibility. Plans, platforms, and partnerships become useful when people understand their role and have the resources and confidence to carry it forward. That lesson connects my departmental leadership, supervision, research development, and professional service.

I am also working to make the connection between my disciplinary expertise and library practice easier to see. Research experience helps me understand the problems researchers face; library colleagues and professional learning help me shape responses that fit our responsibilities and capacity. Continuing that learning, strengthening direct supervisory communication, and defining a coherent scholarly-communication research agenda are priorities for my development.

The reappointment feedback also asks me to communicate my work more clearly. In this evaluation, I have organized the account around the UPP and kept supporting evidence close to the relevant work. As I revise the wider portfolio, I will continue attending to concise summaries, dates, clear descriptions of my role, five-year goals, and colleague proofreading so the presentation helps reviewers understand the substance.
