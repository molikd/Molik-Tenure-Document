## Speech

Thank you to the tenure committee for your continued guidance and the work that entails. I have received some good-natured teasing about the length of this year's portfolio. With a move to Wildcat Scholar on the horizon, as I understand it, I used this packet to show the breadth, depth, and continuing communication my position requires.

I want to begin with two ideas that connect my work as Open Publishing Exchange department head, librarian, associate professor, and Library Lead Research Officer: scholarly communication and sustainability.

By scholarly communication, I mean how people create, publish, share, preserve, find, and reuse knowledge. The Libraries participate throughout that process. We help an author publish a journal article, help a student access an affordable textbook, and help a researcher make data available so someone else can understand and use it. The Open Publishing Exchange brings publishing, repositories, open education, and research-data services together around those responsibilities.

Sustainability is the standard that I apply to that work. In the portfolio, I describe four dimensions. Financial sustainability means having a realistic way to pay for the commitments we make. Operational sustainability means clear responsibilities and workflows, with enough people prepared to carry them out. Technical sustainability means systems we can maintain and information we can preserve. Intellectual sustainability means retaining and sharing the knowledge needed to run those services and improve them.

No single dimension adequately describes OPE's needs. A service can have funding and still be fragile if only one person knows how it works. A new platform can be impressive and still be the wrong choice if we cannot support it over time. My responsibility is to help build services that people can depend on.

That brings me to Tara's evaluation. She asks me to take stock of my commitments and protect a sustainable workload, and to explain specialized work more clearly to people outside the field. I see useful things to work on in both comments.

On workload, I need to apply sustainability to myself. Tara raises a concern about taking on additional projects when others encounter roadblocks. Keeping other work moving can be productive, but I recognize the risk of accumulating commitments. I will review those commitments with administration against departmental priorities, available capacity, and what we can realistically finish. That includes deciding when to defer work and giving colleagues clear ownership, support, and room to lead.

On communication, Tara recognizes that I am a good communicator and asks me to strengthen how I explain specialized ideas. I can do that by checking what context we share and beginning with the problem a service solves. Repository identifiers, for example, help someone reliably find and cite scholarship. My Research Weekly articles on publishing costs and open access show that approach. I want to make it more consistent in everyday conversations.

I also want to address the earlier feedback about my foundation in library science. My route into librarianship has been nontraditional. I do not think that background, by itself, establishes a gap in library knowledge. Publishing, preservation, research-data stewardship, and access to scholarship are library work, and I bring knowledge and experience to those responsibilities. I want the committee to assess that contribution alongside the areas where I can grow.

Conversations with Cliff have reinforced the importance of making my integration into the Libraries visible. I have invested in that work through the Leadership Institute for Academic Librarians, FORCE11, assessment training, and continuing professional reading. I welcome specific guidance about further development. If the committee sees a gap, I ask it to identify the knowledge or skill, its relevance to my responsibilities, and what would demonstrate progress. If a formal qualification is the expectation, I would appreciate that being stated explicitly. Clear expectations would make this feedback actionable.

Shared work is another measure of integration. With Gwen Sibley, I taught data-curation workshops connecting research methods with helping people describe, share, and reuse their data. With Ryan Otto, I worked on repository policy addressing how scholarship is described, discovered, and maintained. These collaborations draw on expertise we each bring, and they deepen my understanding of library practice.

I have also helped develop student roles with named librarian mentors and supported Gwen's proposal development while preserving her ownership. Continuing to learn from colleagues is a career-long responsibility. I intend to pursue that learning with clear objectives and show how it strengthens our services.

A few accomplishments show what this combination of experience and collaboration contributes.

The first is our work with the Journal of Extension. I led New Prairie Press's response to its production-management request for proposals, submitted on October 1, with Ryan involved in the interview process. Since the portfolio was submitted, we have advanced to the next round of interviews. We are still competing for the partnership, but this is meaningful progress: an external scholarly organization is considering the publishing expertise and services our Libraries can provide. It also gives us a concrete setting in which to examine staffing, responsibilities, costs, and whether a partnership can be sustained.

The second is publication reporting. In response to a request from the Office of the Vice President for Research, I developed HAIL and HAIL-local, tools for gathering and connecting publication information. The practical question is straightforward: what scholarship is K-State producing, and how reliably can we describe it? I presented the approach to campus colleagues, and preliminary reports are available. Validation remains part of the work. This is an opportunity to bring library expertise in describing and connecting information into a university-wide need.

The third is research that informs publishing practice. I co-authored *How to write (and review) a Genome Report*, which turns scientific reporting standards into guidance for authors, reviewers, and publishers. Although the subject is genomics, the publishing question is broader: what information must accompany a research result for someone else to assess and reuse it? My contributions to *The Future is FAIR* address related questions about managing and sharing data. AgBioData's Mission and Leadership Award this year also recognized my work in that community. I want those relationships and experiences to strengthen what I contribute here.

Across these examples, my central responsibility remains leading OPE. Publishing opportunities, research tools, and external relationships matter when they help us provide useful services and develop the people who sustain them.

In the coming year, I want to make progress visible through clearer explanations of our work, commitments that match our capacity, and stronger connections across the Libraries through shared work. I am proud of what we are building and of the expertise I contribute. I welcome specific feedback that helps me build on that contribution. Thank you.
