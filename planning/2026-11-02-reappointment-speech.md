## Speech

Thank you for reading my portfolio and for the guidance you have given me. I want to begin with the two ideas that connect the work: scholarly communication and sustainability.

By scholarly communication, I mean how people create, publish, share, preserve, find, and reuse knowledge. The Libraries participate throughout that process. We help an author publish a journal article, help a student access an affordable textbook, and help a researcher make data available so someone else can understand and use it. The Open Publishing Exchange brings publishing, repositories, open education, and research-data services together around those responsibilities.

Sustainability is the standard I want to apply to that work. In the portfolio, I describe four dimensions. Financial sustainability means having a realistic way to pay for the commitments we make. Operational sustainability means clear responsibilities and workflows, with enough people prepared to carry them out. Technical sustainability means systems we can maintain and information we can preserve. Intellectual sustainability means retaining and sharing the knowledge needed to run those services and improve them.

Those dimensions belong together. A service can have funding and still be fragile if only one person knows how it works. A new platform can be impressive and still be the wrong choice if we cannot support it. My responsibility as department head is to help build services that people can depend on over time.

That brings me directly to Tara's evaluation. She asks me to take stock of my commitments and protect a sustainable workload. She also asks me to explain specialized work more clearly to people who do not work in it every day. Both comments are fair, and both belong inside the framework I have just described.

On workload, I need to apply sustainability to myself. Tara also identifies a tendency to respond to a stalled project by taking on something else. I take that seriously. An obstacle needs a decision about scope, timing, or support; adding another commitment can leave the original problem unresolved. Going forward, I will review commitments with Tara against departmental priorities, available capacity, and what we can realistically finish. That means being willing to defer work and give colleagues clear ownership, support, and room to lead.

On communication, I need to begin with the person using the service and the problem we are helping them solve. For example, a discussion of repository identifiers should start with a simple purpose: making sure someone can reliably find and cite a piece of scholarship. Technical detail can follow when it helps a decision. My Research Weekly articles on publishing costs and open access are examples of that effort. I need to make that clarity consistent in everyday conversations as well.

The committee's earlier feedback about my foundation in library science is equally important. Conversations with Cliff have reinforced that I need to show how I am learning library work and becoming more integrated into the Libraries.

I bring experience in research and computing. I also have a great deal to learn about library practice, including the work beyond my own department. That learning may be a career-long responsibility. What I can show now is effort, application, and a willingness to let colleagues' expertise change how I approach a problem.

My professional reading, webinars, and participation in the Coalition for Networked Information and Library Publishing Coalition give me ways to learn from the field. Working alongside colleagues helps me put that learning into practice. With Gwen Sibley, I taught data-curation workshops that connected research methods with helping people describe, share, and reuse their data. Working with Ryan Otto on repository policy has required attention to how scholarship is described, discovered, and maintained. These are library service questions, and I am learning through the people who do that work.

I have also helped develop student roles with named librarian mentors and supported Gwen's proposal development while preserving her ownership of the project. Those experiences reinforce that departmental capacity grows through colleagues' judgment and development. My next step is to make my learning more deliberate with Tara: identify areas of library practice I need to understand better, seek colleagues' guidance, and explain what changes in my work as a result.

A few accomplishments illustrate where that effort is leading.

The first is our work with the Journal of Extension. I led New Prairie Press's response to its production-management request for proposals, submitted on October 1, with Ryan involved in the interview process. Since the portfolio was submitted, we have advanced to the next round of interviews. We are still competing for the partnership, but this is meaningful progress: an external scholarly organization is considering the publishing expertise and services our Libraries can provide. It also gives us a concrete setting in which to examine staffing, responsibilities, costs, and whether a partnership can be sustained.

The second is publication reporting. In response to a request from the Office of the Vice President for Research, I developed HAIL and HAIL-local, tools for gathering and connecting publication information. The practical question is straightforward: what scholarship is K-State producing, and how reliably can we describe it? I presented the approach to campus colleagues, and preliminary reports are available. Validation remains part of the work. This is an opportunity to bring library expertise in describing and connecting information into a university-wide need.

The third is research that informs publishing practice. I co-authored *How to write (and review) a Genome Report*, which turns scientific reporting standards into guidance for authors, reviewers, and publishers. Although the subject is genomics, the publishing question is broader: what information must accompany a research result for someone else to assess and reuse it? My contributions to *The Future is FAIR* address related questions about managing and sharing data. AgBioData's Mission and Leadership Award this year also recognized my work in that community. I want those relationships and experiences to strengthen what I contribute here.

Across these examples, my central responsibility remains leading OPE. Publishing opportunities, research tools, and external relationships matter when they help us provide useful services and develop the people who sustain them.

In the coming year, I want to make progress visible in three ways: clearer explanations of our work, commitments that match our capacity, and deeper understanding of library practice through shared work with colleagues. I am proud of what we are building, and I take seriously the learning and judgment required to carry it forward. Thank you.
